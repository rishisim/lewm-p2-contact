\begin{abstract}
Comparisons of adaptive refinement in physical world models are confounded when routing overhead and fixed-depth baselines are charged under different arithmetic. We introduce a matched-counted-\flops{} evaluation protocol with a frozen compute ledger and use it to test whether a history-conditioned allocator can improve one-step latent prediction by redistributing refinement. The allocator observes only information available before the target latent---past and present latents, current action blocks, predictions, and updates---and its feature and scoring overhead is charged to the ledger. We evaluate one frozen artifact/population pair in each of two simulated visual-manipulation settings. On 1,600 fresh Cube episodes, allocation improves raw latent mean-squared error (MSE) by 0.1968\% and native-whitened MSE by 0.6125\% relative to the strongest fixed-depth envelope, with positive multiplicity-adjusted lower bounds. A PushT four-depth pilot is negative under its two-endpoint rule; a pilot-generated binary rule is then locked and confirmed on 240 disjoint episodes, improving raw MSE by 2.2521\% and fit-whitened MSE by 0.5346\%. The frozen Cube allocator retains four of six shift endpoint claims. These results show that transition-conditioned refinement can improve next-latent fidelity for the evaluated frozen artifacts at fixed declared arithmetic. They do not establish contact recognition, cross-environment transfer, latency, energy, planning, or control benefits.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Evaluation protocols for adaptive computation in embodied world models must separate \emph{where} computation is assigned from \emph{how much} computation is spent. Physical-interaction trajectories make that distinction salient: free motion, contact, policy-induced discontinuities, and nearly settled states can occur in one trajectory, yet many latent predictors use a fixed inference depth. This paper studies a focused question: \emph{can a world model use information available before the target latent to reassign a fixed refinement budget across transitions and thereby improve next-latent prediction?}

This is a question about allocation, not budget reduction. If adaptive and fixed systems use different arithmetic, any quality difference can be explained by total compute. We count the base predictor, refiners, feature construction, and gate scoring, then compare against the strongest feasible mixture of fixed depths at the same expected counted \flops{}. The comparison asks whether conditioning refinement on transition history improves loss relative to an outcome-independent fixed-depth envelope.

The distinction is scientifically useful even without a systems claim. A matched-compute prediction result tests a mechanism---history-conditioned refinement---without confounding it with a larger model or a larger inference budget. It can justify later work on sparse kernels, batching, or controllers, but it does not itself imply lower latency, lower energy, or better actions. We therefore separate predictive fidelity from implementation speed and decision utility throughout.

We evaluate a stagewise latent predictor in two visual manipulation environments: single-cube robot manipulation and contact-rich planar PushT. In Cube, the full four-depth model, allocator, whitening transform, and thresholds are frozen before a 1,600-episode confirmation and three 3,000-episode one-factor shifts. In PushT, environment-specific fitting produces a four-depth pilot that fails its prespecified two-endpoint criterion. A read-only analysis of that consumed pilot motivates a simpler binary rule; the rule is locked and tested on 240 new episodes. The central result is therefore two artifact/population-specific confirmations, the second of a pilot-generated hypothesis, at matched expected counted \flops{}.

Physical-interaction trajectories are a plausible setting for allocation because the value of another dynamics update can depend on recent motion, action changes, and whether the current refinement is still large relative to the predicted state. These signals are present before the target arrives even though realized prediction error is not. The gate is not supervised with contact, geometry, reward, or success, so the experiment tests predictive allocation on physical trajectories rather than identification of a physical event.

Two endpoints are used because latent scale can hide different failure modes. Raw MSE weights the coordinates as represented by the frozen model. Whitened MSE normalizes correlated directions using a transform fixed without confirmation outcomes, making low-variance directions more visible. Requiring both endpoints to pass is stricter than selecting whichever metric favors the method. It is also why the negative PushT pilot remains scientifically informative: its raw gain cannot cancel its whitened failure.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item a reusable matched-counted-\flops{} evaluation protocol with a frozen operation ledger, endpoint-specific fixed-depth envelopes, charged routing overhead, and runnable transition-independent controls;
  \item a history-conditioned allocator for frozen stagewise latent predictors that estimates adjacent-depth raw and whitened gains without the target latent, with gate overhead included in the declared \flops{} ledger;
  \item role-separated evidence from two simulated manipulation artifact/population pairs: a frozen four-depth Cube confirmation and a fresh confirmation of a pilot-generated binary PushT rule;
  \item a bounded evidence record retaining the negative PushT pilot, partial Cube shift robustness, whitening sensitivity, one-artifact limitation, and absent latency, planning, and control evidence.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Adaptive depth.}
Adaptive Computation Time, Universal Transformers, and PonderNet learn when recurrent computation should stop \citep{graves2016adaptive,dehghani2019universal,banino2021pondernet}. Early-exit and conditional-routing systems similarly stop or skip work for individual inputs \citep{teerapittayanon2016branchynet,bolukbasi2017adaptive,wang2018skipnet,wu2018blockdrop}, while recent systems allocate depth at token level \citep{raposo2024mixture,bae2025mixture}. Our target differs in both domain and comparison: we route latent physical transitions and test against a fixed-depth loss envelope after charging the allocator itself.

\paragraph{Physical world models.}
Latent dynamics support prediction, imagination, and behavior learning in PlaNet and Dreamer \citep{hafner2019planet,hafner2020dreamer}; transformer and diffusion systems extend this line \citep{micheli2023iris,alonso2024diamond}. LeWorldModel supplies the frozen predictor family \citep{maes2026leworldmodel}, while stable-worldmodel supplies the standardized environment and evaluation layer \citep{maes2026stableworldmodel}. Our physical tasks originate in OGBench's robot manipulation suite \citep{park2025ogbench} and the PushT benchmark introduced with Diffusion Policy \citep{chi2023diffusion}. Closely related concurrent studies examine learned early exit in text-interactive worlds \citep{lu2026loopwm} and fixed exit-depth regimes across control tasks \citep{sivasankar2026adaptive}. We complement them with learned per-transition routing and exact matched-ledger tests. Adaptive rollout horizon and sparse imagination allocate planning resources rather than single-transition refinement \citep{bhatia2022adaptive,chun2026sparse}; our planning bridge does not support a planning claim.

Unlike contemporaneous visuo-tactile models that explicitly represent contact state and test physical fidelity or planning \citep{higuera2026visuotactile,ma2026feelworld}, we allocate compute from latent/action history without claiming a contact model.

\section{History-Conditioned Allocation}
\label{sec:method}

\paragraph{Stagewise prediction.}
Let \(h_t=(z_{t-H+1:t},a_{t-H+1:t})\) contain only observed latent and action history. A frozen base predictor produces \(b_t=f_0(h_t)\), and a stagewise solver produces
\begin{equation}
  \hat z^{(1)}_{t+1}=b_t+u^{(1)}_t,\qquad
  \hat z^{(d)}_{t+1}=\hat z^{(d-1)}_{t+1}+u^{(d)}_t,\quad d=2,\ldots,D .
  \label{eq:refinement}
\end{equation}
Cube uses a depth-one anchor and three depth-specific residual adapters. PushT uses a shared recurrent block with iteration embeddings. Both expose depths one through four; the PushT confirmation later restricts this frozen mechanism to depths one and two.

\paragraph{Non-anticipatory gate.}
At reached stage \(s\), the gate builds \(x_{t,s}\) from three latent states, three actions, the current prediction, the latest update, and 11 norms, distances, cosines, and temporal-change summaries. Cube and PushT use 1,046 and 1,001 features, respectively, because their blocked actions differ in dimension. The gate never receives \(z_{t+1}\), a future action, contact label, reward, success, or simulator geometry. We therefore call it \emph{non-anticipatory} or \emph{history-conditioned}; we do not claim causal identification.

The vector features preserve the latent and action coordinates that a linear head can use directly. The scalar summaries expose magnitudes and alignments that would otherwise require multiplicative interactions: norms of the prediction and update, cosine relationships, distances between recent latent states, and temporal changes in those quantities. Heads are stage-specific because a score at depth one and a score after two refinements operate on different reached populations. They are fitted only on rows that reached the corresponding stage under the frozen preceding decisions.

Two stage-specific ridge heads estimate standardized raw and whitened gain from one more step. The conservative score and routing rule are
\begin{equation}
 q_{t,s}=\min\{g^{\raw}_{t,s},g^{\white}_{t,s}\},\qquad
 \text{continue at stage }s\ \Longleftrightarrow\ q_{t,s}>\tau_s .
 \label{eq:gate}
\end{equation}
Later stages are evaluated only for transitions that continue. Figure~\ref{fig:allocator} makes the information boundary and accounting explicit.

Training targets for the heads are realized reductions in next-step loss between adjacent exits, standardized using fit-role statistics. At deployment, the minimum of the two predicted gains implements a conservative conjunction: a transition continues only when the learned rule expects improvement in both coordinate systems above the selected threshold. The heads do not update online, and an observed target never changes a later decision within the evaluation cohort.

\paragraph{Endpoints and matched compute.}
For episode \(e\), raw error averages squared latent residuals over coordinates and transitions. Whitened error applies a transform \(W\) frozen before evaluation. Benefit is comparator minus adaptive MSE, so positive values favor allocation. Episodes, not transitions, are the inferential units.

More explicitly, for transition set \(T_e\) and latent dimension \(p\),
\begin{equation}
 L_e^{\raw}(d)=\frac{1}{|T_e|}\sum_{t\in T_e}\frac{1}{p}
 \left\|z_{t+1}-\hat z^{(d)}_{t+1}\right\|_2^2 .
 \label{eq:rawloss}
\end{equation}
The whitened endpoint replaces the residual by \(W(z_{t+1}-\hat z_{t+1})\). Cube uses a PlanOracle-native transform estimated from 120 fit episodes, before selection or confirmation, with eigenvalue floor \(\max(10^{-8},10^{-4}\tilde\lambda_+)\), where \(\tilde\lambda_+\) is the median positive covariance eigenvalue. PushT uses a covariance transform estimated only on its fit role, with a prespecified eigenvalue floor ratio of \(10^{-3}\). Under Cube shifts, the unchanged Cube matrix is called \emph{fixed-whitened}: it is not re-estimated to make a shifted population easier to evaluate. For metric \(m\), we report
\begin{equation}
 \benefit_m=L_m^{\rm comparator}-L_m^{\rm adaptive},
 \label{eq:benefit}
\end{equation}
and form episode loss before any bootstrap or population average. Relative effects divide \(\benefit_m\) by comparator MSE, so raw and whitened effects share an axis only after normalization.

For \(N\) transitions, the adaptive ledger is
\begin{equation}
 C_{\rm adapt}=NC_{\rm base}+NC_{\rm mandatory}
   +R_{\rm ref}C_{\rm ref}+R_{\rm gate}C_{\rm gate}.
 \label{eq:ledger}
\end{equation}
Comparisons, indexing, memory traffic, simulator work, and pixel encoding are excluded rather than assigned fictional \flops{}. For fixed depths \(\ell<u\) bracketing \(C_{\rm adapt}\), we choose
\begin{equation}
 w_{\ell u}=\frac{C_{\rm adapt}-C_\ell}{C_u-C_\ell},\qquad
 L^{\rm mix}_{\ell u}=(1-w_{\ell u})L_\ell+w_{\ell u}L_u ,
\end{equation}
then use the feasible pair with the smallest full-cohort loss for each endpoint. The selected pair and weight are held fixed during episode resampling. This analytic envelope exactly matches expected counted \flops{} and is deliberately strong, but it is not an executable fractional schedule. Runnable randomized controls are secondary. Full operation ledgers and control results are in \cref{app:compute,app:controls}.

\paragraph{Interpretation and runnable controls.}
The envelope converts the allocator's complete charge into the strongest endpoint-specific point on the fixed-depth loss curve. Positive benefit therefore identifies useful allocation at equal expected counted \flops{}, not an executable fractional schedule or equal latency. Secondary seeded fixed-depth mixtures and within-episode call permutations change which transitions receive depth while matching or preserving calls. Their favorable existing results provide an executable check on transition conditioning but are not additional confirmatory families.

\section{Experimental Design}

\paragraph{Physical tasks.}
Both tasks are rendered visual dynamics benchmarks exposed through stable-worldmodel \citep{maes2026stableworldmodel}. Cube is the single-object OGBench manipulation environment \citep{park2025ogbench}: a MuJoCo robot arm acts through contact to move a cube to a target position. We evaluate 224\(\times\)224 images under a scripted PlanOracle with 25-dimensional five-step action blocks. PushT is a 2D Pymunk manipulation task \citep{chi2023diffusion}: a circular end-effector uses relative motion to push and rotate a T-shaped block toward a target pose. Its WeakPolicy keeps sampled actions near the block; five two-dimensional actions form each 10-dimensional block. In both tasks, the five simulator sub-actions collectively form the current command applied between \(z_t\) and \(z_{t+1}\) and are known when predicting \(z_{t+1}\); no later command is exposed. The predictor sees pixels and actions, while the allocator sees frozen latent/action history and no simulator state, contact count, reward, or geometry.

\paragraph{Cube.}
Gate heads are fitted on 240 episodes and thresholds selected on 120 disjoint episodes. The complete four-depth package is then frozen. The main confirmation contains 1,600 fresh PlanOracle episodes (60,800 transitions) from \texttt{swm/OGBCube-v0}. Co-primary endpoints are raw and native-whitened MSE; both Bonferroni-adjusted one-sided lower bounds must exceed zero. We use 20,000 paired episode bootstraps. Without refitting, the same package is tested under three one-factor shifts---MarkovOracle, doubled action noise, and 0.1 random-action probability---with 3,000 fresh episodes each and six multiplicity-controlled regime-by-endpoint claims.

The native population uses action noise 0.1, no random actions, history length three, and 38 evaluated transitions per episode. The one-factor shifts alter only the named factor while retaining all weights, features, thresholds, whitening, and accounting. Their six simultaneous lower bounds use level \(0.05/6\); no rule is retuned for a shift.

\paragraph{PushT.}
For \texttt{swm/PushT-v1}, a 120-episode fit role trains the recurrent refiner, whitening transform, and ridge heads; 40 disjoint episodes select a four-depth candidate. An 80-episode pilot requires favorable raw and fit-whitened point benefits. It fails because the whitened endpoint is negative. A read-only analysis of those consumed arrays suggests retaining only the frozen stage-one decision, yielding a mandatory depth-one/optional depth-two rule. That hypothesis, threshold, metrics, 240 fresh seeds, and rejection rule are sealed before a disjoint confirmation. Both co-primary simultaneous lower bounds must be positive under 20,000 paired episode bootstraps. All PushT fit, selection, pilot, and confirmation episodes under the WeakPolicy have zero task success; the study concerns prediction on those trajectories.

PushT uses history length three and 18 evaluated transitions per episode. Its shared recurrent block has 256 hidden units and a 16-dimensional iteration embedding; the selected checkpoint contains 323,840 trainable parameters. Six gate candidates cross ridge regularization 1 or 10 with fit-score quantiles 0.45, 0.60, or 0.75; the selection role chooses regularization 10 and quantile 0.75. The pilot-generated binary protocol preserves the original stage-one heads and freezes threshold 0.0365982. It computes no later-stage features or scores, an important detail for the new exact ledger.

\paragraph{Role separation.}
No evaluation cohort described as fresh is used to fit a refiner, gate, whitening transform, or threshold. All intervals resample whole episodes with the same bootstrap indices for adaptive and comparator losses. Hashes, timestamps, detailed cohorts, and secondary diagnostics appear in the supplement.

\paragraph{Decision rules and diagnostics.}
Cube and binary PushT confirmations each have a two-endpoint family. A study passes only if both one-sided Bonferroni-adjusted lower bounds exceed zero; favorable routing correlations cannot override a failed endpoint. We also report ordinary two-sided percentile intervals, relative effects, allocation histograms, and Spearman correlation between the frozen score and realized combined gain on rows that reach each stage. Correlations are mechanism diagnostics rather than independent confirmation tests because realized gain uses the target latent.

The primary hypotheses are artifact- and population-specific. For frozen Cube PlanOracle, \(H_{\rm Cube}\) requires \(\benefit_{\raw}>0\) and \(\benefit_{\white}>0\) against their strongest exact-counted-\flops{} envelopes. For the fresh PushT binary cohort, \(H_{\rm PushT}\) requires the same conjunction under the fit-frozen whitening transform. Rejecting both null families supports two fitted instances of the allocation mechanism; it does not estimate a common cross-environment or retraining-stable effect. The shift family then asks a separate question about the already-frozen Cube package, and the exploratory bridge asks whether a one-step effect survives composition.

The Cube shift study is intentionally stricter: all six regime-by-endpoint claims share one family. We label robustness \emph{partial} when some but not all simultaneous lower bounds are positive. Exploratory composition and planning analyses use already-consumed data and have no path to upgrade the primary result. This hierarchy prevents a favorable secondary slice from changing a prespecified terminal decision.

\section{Results}

\paragraph{Cube confirmation.}
The frozen four-depth allocator passes both co-primary tests. Raw MSE is 0.00343354 versus 0.00344031 for the strongest matched-counted-\flops{} envelope, a benefit of \(6.7690\times10^{-6}\) (95\% interval \([5.4240,8.1630]\times10^{-6}\)), or 0.1968\%. Native-whitened MSE is 0.0987642 versus 0.0993728, a benefit of \(6.0862\times10^{-4}\) (95\% interval \([5.1356,7.0843]\times10^{-4}\)), or 0.6125\%. The displayed lower endpoints also serve as simultaneous one-sided lower bounds. At 4,332,936,120,435 counted \flops{} per arm, routing assigns 50,973, 7,267, 1,773, and 787 transitions to depths one through four, averaging 1.2167 refiner calls versus 1.2530 gate-adjusted comparator-equivalent calls. Score--realized-gain Spearman correlations remain positive at all reached stages (0.263, 0.171, 0.141).

The histogram corresponds to 83.8\% of transitions stopping at depth one, 12.0\% at depth two, 2.9\% at depth three, and 1.3\% at depth four. Thus the effect is not produced by a nearly fixed depth-two system: most transitions stop immediately while a small tail receives multiple refinements. The stage-two and stage-three correlations are computed only over 9,827 and 2,560 reached rows, respectively, and should not be compared directly with the stage-one value over all 60,800 transitions. Their shared positive direction is nevertheless consistent with the allocator ranking additional refinement more effectively than random assignment.

\paragraph{PushT pilot and confirmation.}
The four-depth pilot is retained as a negative result: raw benefit is 0.0039098, but fit-whitened benefit is \(-0.0003099\), failing the two-endpoint rule. On the later 240-episode binary confirmation, raw MSE falls from 0.1213907 to 0.1186569, a benefit of 0.0027338 (95\% interval [0.0024323, 0.0030399]), or 2.2521\%. Fit-whitened MSE falls from 0.2935357 to 0.2919665, a benefit of 0.0015692 (95\% interval [0.0012688, 0.0018738]), or 0.5346\%. Both simultaneous lower bounds are positive. Adaptive and comparator arms each use exactly 307,585,049,440 counted \flops{}. The rule sends 3,265 transitions to depth one and 1,055 to depth two; the stage-one score has Spearman 0.458 with combined realized gain.

The pilot's negative whitened effect has exploratory interval [\(-0.0012590\), 0.0006292], whereas its favorable raw interval is [0.0027287, 0.0050886]. The disagreement is precisely why the conjunction rule was specified. The four-depth histogram [1,089, 42, 46, 263] also reveals an unusually heavy depth-four tail. Removing later routing stages after viewing that outcome is hypothesis generation, not a repair of the pilot. Only the disjoint binary cohort supports the pilot-generated binary rule, and it does not rehabilitate the original four-depth candidate.

\paragraph{Frozen shifts and boundary tests.}
The unchanged Cube allocator retains four of six multiplicity-controlled shift claims: raw and fixed-whitened benefits remain positive under doubled action noise, while fixed-whitened benefits remain positive under Markov and random-action shifts. Markov raw benefit is negative and random-action raw benefit is inconclusive. This is partial, not universal, robustness.

Five-step composition on consumed Cube data is directionally favorable but exploratory. A 20-start, 64-candidate planning bridge is uninformative: no start meets the prespecified candidate-diversity rule, both rollout intervals cross zero, and adaptive and matched selections differ on only one start. Synchronized timings are also unfavorable to the current adaptive implementation. These results bound the confirmed claim to next-latent fidelity at matched expected counted arithmetic.

\paragraph{Result boundary.}
For the two evaluated frozen artifact/population pairs, selective refinement improves both next-latent endpoints after paying for routing overhead in the declared ledger. The experiments do not establish that the gate detects contact, that the effect survives retraining, that it transfers across environments without fitting, or that it improves latency or actions.

\section{Discussion}

The two confirmations answer the allocation question narrowly: for these frozen artifacts and populations, choosing \emph{which} transitions receive refinement can improve prediction at fixed expected counted arithmetic. Positive score--gain correlations provide a mechanism diagnostic, while fresh episode-level endpoints provide the statistical test. Cube is the stronger anchor because the entire four-depth package is frozen before a large confirmation. PushT uses environment-specific fitting and confirms only the pilot-generated binary hypothesis.

The effect sizes should be interpreted in context. Cube's sub-percent changes are small in average latent MSE, but their paired episode intervals are narrow and exclude zero after multiplicity adjustment. These intervals resolve evaluation-population uncertainty for the frozen artifact; they do not show retraining stability or task importance. PushT's raw relative effect is larger, yet its whitening scale, model fit, and trajectory population differ; pooling the percentages would be misleading. The common observation is qualitative: a score derived without the target latent ranks realized refinement benefit well enough to beat the declared fixed-depth envelope.

Raw and whitened agreement strengthens that interpretation but does not make the metric task-aware. Whitening changes the geometry of prediction error, not the downstream value of a latent direction. The PushT sensitivity analysis further shows that this geometry depends on regularization of small covariance eigenvalues. Freezing the transform prevents outcome-driven metric choice in the confirmation; reporting the sensitivity prevents that procedural validity from being mistaken for metric invariance.

Matched-counted-\flops{} prediction is useful even without latency or control evidence because it removes one alternative explanation. Within the declared ledger, the improvement is not explained by a larger arithmetic budget: allocator overhead is paid from that ledger. This establishes an empirical target for future systems work---preserve transition selection while making routing hardware-efficient---and for decision studies on behaviorally informative candidate sets.

The comparator is conservative in loss space: it interpolates between feasible fixed depths and can choose a different pair for each metric. Its weakness is operational because fractional expected depth does not specify an executable schedule. The runnable controls preserve or slightly exceed the relevant calls and support the same direction, but they remain transition-independent baselines rather than alternative learned routers. Counted arithmetic is reproducible from the graph, whereas latency depends on implementation, device, batching, and synchronization. The present dynamic implementation is slower; efficient deployment requires a separate systems study.

The negative and mixed results prevent overgeneralization. The PushT pilot shows that adding more routing stages can help one metric while harming another. The Cube shifts show that a frozen allocator does not retain every endpoint. The planning bridge shows that small prediction changes need not produce measurable decision changes, especially with tied candidates. These are not side notes; they define what has and has not been learned.

Next work should separately test hardware-efficient routing, closed-loop outcomes on informative candidate sets, and transfer with features and thresholds frozen across more bodies, contacts, and policies. The negative pilot also suggests treating each later routing stage as an added hypothesis: later heads see smaller policy-selected subsets, whereas the confirmed binary rule has a simpler ledger and fewer opportunities for compounding selection.

\section{Limitations}

The evidence is restricted to one frozen trained artifact per environment, one native Cube population, three one-factor Cube shifts, and one zero-success PushT population. Episode resampling measures uncertainty over evaluation episodes for those artifacts; it does not measure variation from retraining the predictor, refiner, gate, or whitening transform. PushT fitting and threshold selection are environment-specific, so this is not Cube-to-PushT transfer. Cube effects are below one percent of comparator MSE; the larger PushT raw percentage occurs on a different scale and should not be pooled.

The exact comparator is an expectation-level fixed-depth envelope, not an executable fractional schedule. The ledger excludes memory traffic, indexing, synchronization, simulation, and pixel encoding. Measured latency does not favor the adaptive path, and energy is unmeasured. The PushT whitened result is valid for its prespecified \(10^{-3}\) covariance floor but is not invariant to smaller post-outcome floors. The binary PushT hypothesis is post-pilot at the program level even though its confirmation cohort is fresh.

The gate receives no contact or geometry labels, and score--gain correlation does not establish that it identifies a physical event. The latent endpoints are not task-aware, all PushT episodes have zero success, and the planning bridge has no informative candidate set; no physical-state, planning, or closed-loop evidence establishes behavioral utility. Features inherit the latent coordinates and may not transfer across encoders. We neither compare every budgeted router nor jointly train allocation, so the conclusion is restricted to the declared comparators and frozen stagewise predictors.

\section{Conclusion}

Across two frozen visual-manipulation artifact/population pairs, history-conditioned depth allocation lowers raw and whitened one-step latent MSE relative to a fixed-depth envelope at matched expected counted \flops{}. The result supports a narrow allocation mechanism: under the declared ledger, where optional refinement is assigned can affect predictive fidelity. It does not show that the router recognizes contact or geometry, transfers without fitting, survives model retraining, reduces latency or energy, or improves planning and control.

\subsection{Runnable controls and reproducibility specification}
\label{app:controls}

The primary comparator is analytic. Secondary executable controls use already-frozen confirmation arrays and are descriptive rather than additional confirmatory families. Cube's seeded depth-one/two schedule uses 103,565 more counted \flops{} than adaptive and favors adaptive in raw MSE by \(7.5655\times10^{-6}\) (95\% interval \([5.8645,9.2987]\times10^{-6}\)). Independently permuting the realized depth histogram within each episode favors adaptive by \(5.5545\times10^{-6}\) raw (\([4.2226,6.9351]\times10^{-6}\)) and \(5.9536\times10^{-4}\) native-whitened MSE (\([5.3835,6.5368]\times10^{-4}\)). For binary PushT, within-episode call randomization favors adaptive by 0.0027573 raw ([0.0024374, 0.0030847]) and 0.0016274 fit-whitened MSE ([0.0013812, 0.0018812]). These controls preserve or slightly exceed calls but do not compare against another learned router.

The causal feature vector is the concatenation of three latent histories, three fit-normalized current action blocks, the current prediction, the latest update, and 11 summaries: prediction, update, and latest-history norms; relative update norm; prediction-to-history distance; update--prediction and update--history-gap cosines; two consecutive latent-change norms; and two consecutive action-change norms. This gives 1,046 Cube and 1,001 PushT causal features before the Cube stage indicator. Cube uses dual ridge heads with regularization 0.01 and fit-score quantile 0.85; its raw and whitened targets and feature normalization are fit-role quantities. PushT uses regularization 10 and quantile 0.75. Its recurrent refiner is trained with AdamW (learning rate \(3\times10^{-4}\), weight decay \(10^{-4}\)), batch size 256, gradient-norm cap 1.0, at most 250 epochs, and a fixed every-tenth-fit-episode validation partition; checkpoint selection minimizes validation prediction loss. The loss adds \(10^{-4}\) times mean squared update magnitude. All multiply-add ledgers count one multiplication plus one accumulation or bias contribution as two \flops{}.

\subsection{Negative pilot, whitening sensitivity, and inference details}

The PushT four-depth pilot contains 80 episodes and 1,440 transitions. Raw benefit is 0.0039098 (95\% exploratory interval [0.0027287, 0.0050886]), while fit-whitened benefit is \(-0.0003099\) (interval [\(-0.0012590\), 0.0006292]); it therefore fails. Its depth histogram is [1,089, 42, 46, 263]. The binary confirmation uses a fresh 240 episodes and 4,320 transitions. Its histogram is [3,265, 1,055].

The frozen PushT transform floors covariance eigenvalues at \(10^{-3}\) of the reference scale. Re-estimating covariance on confirmation targets after outcomes are known is invalid as a replacement endpoint, but exposes sensitivity: binary benefit is 0.0013365 at \(10^{-3}\), 0.0008800 at \(3\times10^{-4}\), and \(-0.0001743\) at \(10^{-4}\).

All bootstrap procedures resample episodes and preserve adaptive--comparator pairing. Confirmation uses 20,000 replicates and one-sided Bonferroni adjustment over two co-primary endpoints. Cube shifts use 20,000 common draws and a six-claim family. Fixed-depth losses are averaged at the episode level before analytic interpolation. Because raw and whitened curves can order differently, the strongest bracketing depth pair may differ by endpoint.
